Candidate new hires, their presence in USPTO, and all USPTO-LinkedIn inventors

There are four parts in this report.

  1. Universe of candidate focal new hires

    • The first part characterizes the universe sample of candidate focal new hires.
    • It is the broadest sample of new hires we can study.
    • The only restrictions are that they (1) start a new position during 2021–23 and (2) enter a position under occupation code 17 (Architecture and Engineering occupations) or occupation code 19 (Life, Physical, and Social Science occupations).
    • One important but unpleasant message is that Revelio’s occupation classifications are quite messy.
    • A large share of records fall into unexpected occupations, such as historians and astronomers, reflecting Revelio's imperfect construction of standardized occupation variables.

  2. Match rates to US patents data

    • The second part asks to what extent the universe sample can be matched to the US patents database (USPTO).
    • I call the share of candidate new hires who also appear in USPTO the match rate.
    • The main goal is to characterize heterogeneous match rates across countries, industries, occupations, and seniority levels.

  3. USPTO-LinkedIn matched sample of candidate new hires

    • The third part characterizes the USPTO-LinkedIn matched sample of candidate new hires.
    • It contains only the subsample of the universe sample that can be matched to the US patents database.
    • One key message is that the matched sample is very different from the universe sample in its country, industry, occupation, and seniority composition.

  4. USPTO-LinkedIn matched inventors

    • The fourth part characterizes the full sample of inventors in the US patents database.
    • Unlike the previous three parts, these inventors are not necessarily new hires.
    • I include the full USPTO-LinkedIn matched sample, regardless of whether an inventor reports a new position during 2021–23 under occupation code 17 or 19.
    • This part has two purposes:
      1. To better understand USPTO-LinkedIn inventors, even when they are not new hires and therefore never appear in our analysis sample.
      2. To examine the self-reported job titles of USPTO-LinkedIn inventors in case we construct the analysis sample using job titles rather than ONET occupation codes.
        • Previous reports show that Revelio’s occupation classifications are quite messy.
        • This suggests that we may want to construct the final analysis sample using users’ self-reported job titles instead of Revelio’s occupation variables.
    • One key, and pleasant, message is that although individual ONET codes are quite messy, they can still capture almost all inventors—especially in our baseline BioPharma industry.
